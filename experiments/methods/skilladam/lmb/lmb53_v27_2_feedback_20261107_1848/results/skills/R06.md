# Live Mathematical MCQ Heuristics

## Workflow
1. Parse the claim before reading the choices. Write down what is asserted (existence, uniqueness, a regularity class, a classification list, an equivalence of several conditions) together with the exact hypotheses and quantifiers: "for all", "there exists", "exactly", "at most", "if and only if".
2. Calibrate the strength of the result. Decide what the underlying theorem actually delivers: which regularity or exponent, whether a full equivalence or only one implication is proved, and whether the hypotheses cover the whole family or only a restricted subfamily or a set of excluded cases.
3. Compare every option against that calibration, one distinguishing feature at a time. Name the single feature separating each option from its nearest neighbor (an exponent formula, a divisor condition, an added converse, an excluded case, a regularity upgrade) and mark it supported, unsupported, or a weakening. Then order the options by logical strength wherever they differ only in strength: which one implies which, as when one option controls the full gradient while another controls a single partial derivative, when one states a sharp exponent and another a range, or when one asserts mutual equivalence and another only one implication. The answer is the strongest claim the hypotheses actually reach: a true but strictly weaker variant is still wrong, and upgrading an established one-way implication into an equivalence is wrong even though it looks more complete. Reject options with explicit reasons; never stop at the first plausible one.
4. Weigh the catch-all option explicitly, after everything else. An option stating that a stronger result than the listed ones can be proved is not a lazy fallback. Whenever such an option is present, test every concrete option for the defect that keeps it from being the theorem's full content: a weakened or narrowed form of the claim (a single partial derivative where the theorem controls the whole gradient, a subfamily where it covers the full range, one implication where the classification supplies two directions), or a clause the result does not supply (a converse, an equality characterization, an exact constant, a decomposition of a prescribed order). If every concrete option carries such a defect, choose the catch-all. The same verdict applies when the concrete options vary in which features of one statement they assert instead of nesting inside one another — one keeps a sharp constant but narrows the parameter range, another widens the range but renames the object, a third trades a stated hypothesis for an extra conclusion. Options whose trade-offs point in different directions are pairwise incomparable, so no one of them can be the theorem's full content; the detail separating them (an index placement, a constant inside an exponent, which hypothesis or which function appears) is exactly what the hypotheses let you neither confirm nor refute, and such variants were produced by perturbing a single statement, so guessing among them is worse than taking the catch-all.
5. Format. Output exactly one label inside <answer>...</answer> and nothing else.

## Error Avoidance
- Do not default to the option with the most clauses. Converse claims, "every such case occurs" additions, exact exponent formulas, and silently dropped hypotheses are standard distractor moves; justify each added clause independently or reject the option.
- Do not default to the hedged option either. When the result does establish the stronger claim (an embedding, a full equivalence, a complete classification, a second conclusion alongside the first), an option weakening it to "need not" or "for some" — or one that simply states fewer of the theorem's conclusions and drops one outright — is the distractor. Judge each option by its full content rather than its length: the longest option is not right by default, and neither is the shortest.
- In piecewise or split-regime statements, match each regime separately, including whether a boundary value is included (< versus <=) and which regime receives the sharper exponent.
- When options differ only by a restriction on a parameter (dividing one integer versus twice it, any divisor versus an even one), the correct option uses exactly the restriction the proof delivers; a stronger or weaker restriction is a distractor.
