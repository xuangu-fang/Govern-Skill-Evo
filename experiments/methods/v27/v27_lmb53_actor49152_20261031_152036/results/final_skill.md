# Live Mathematical MCQ Heuristics

## Option Comparison
- Compare all options before committing. The correct choice is often the strongest statement justified by the question, while nearby distractors are weaker, overstrong, or miss an equality case.
- Track exact quantifiers such as "there exists", "for every", "if and only if", and "exactly when".

## Theorem-Level Precision
- Check whether an option weakens the conclusion by dropping a characterization, equality clause, or full equivalence.
- Check whether an option overstates the theorem by upgrading regularity, removing scale restrictions, or changing an existential statement into a universal one.

## Hypotheses
- Verify the hypotheses and domain carefully. Distractors often keep the theorem shape but alter the required assumptions.
- Pay close attention to equality cases, extremal conditions, and whether a result applies to the full family or only a restricted subfamily.

## Choice-Level Distinctions
- When a question asks for the strongest, most complete, valid, or equivalent statement and an option expressly says that one of the remaining options is correct but a stronger result can be proved (or expressly says the listed statements are not exhaustive), treat that option as a substantive candidate rather than as empty or merely meta. Compare each concrete option as a whole against the exact requested sharp formulation. If no concrete option visibly states the full requested formulation without an unsupported restriction, overreach, or weakened component—such as 'there exists' versus a specified object, 'degree at most' versus the exact degree, an altered coefficient, an altered boundary condition, or a different quantifier order—and the meta-option expressly claims that a stronger result is provable, select the meta-option rather than a concrete option that only has one exact-looking component. A concrete option that merely looks stronger by adding uniformity (for example, replacing a parameter that may depend on the point with one fixed parameter) or by upgrading a quantifier can be an unsupported overreach; such an option does not state the strongest provable equivalence when it exceeds the hypotheses or omits the full requested condition. Likewise, do not treat an existential 'there exists a polynomial of degree at most ...' or a formula with a detailed exponent as the exact theorem when the quantifier or degree is weakened. Do not choose the meta-option merely because it mentions strength, merely because a concrete option is lengthy, or for an imperfection outside the requested quantification, equality, degree, boundary, or equivalence; retain a concrete option when it visibly carries the full requested characterization or equivalence without such an alteration. Preserve the stated hypotheses, quantifier scope, domains, and the distinction between a weaker valid statement and the requested characterization.
- When options state logical relations among several labeled properties, parse each option as a directed implication graph before choosing. Do not upgrade a stated equivalence plus a one-way implication into a full all-pairs equivalence merely because the latter sounds stronger; an option that adds a converse or an extra implication requires explicit support. Select the option whose exact directions, equivalences, and quantifier scope match the hypotheses.
- When blow-up or regularity choices differ by an exact critical exponent versus a relaxed epsilon variant, such as exponent 2 versus 2−ε, or by the full gradient versus one partial derivative, treat these as separate assertions. Do not assume the relaxed exponent or the single-component norm implies or is stronger than the exact full-gradient criterion; choose the formulation whose exponent and norm directly match the sharp criterion, unless the hypotheses explicitly establish an equivalence.
- When a question asks for the strongest statement about a full class of functions or operators, and a choice adds a restrictive hypothesis such as frequency support or bandlimitedness while giving a positive estimate, compare the quantifier scope before choosing. Do not treat a conditional bound on a restricted subclass as stronger than an unrestricted statement about the whole class; unless the question expressly asks for the restricted or localized version, do not select a detailed formula that applies only under an extra unrequested restriction.
- In complete-list or classification questions, separate the claim that every object has one of the listed forms from a converse that every permitted parameter value in a listed family actually occurs. Treat such a converse as an additional universal-existence claim requiring independent support. When two choices otherwise match and one adds such a converse, especially 'conversely, every such divisor or parameter actually occurs', select the version without it unless the hypotheses or the requested classification expressly establish that all permitted values occur. Do not infer occurrence from a numerical divisibility condition or from the fact that the value is allowed.
- When options contrast a positive structural property such as an embedding or injectivity with a claim that a natural map need not be injective or can identify classes, do not infer the negative claim solely from a quotient, a finite-index subgroup, or a many-to-one intermediate construction. Wording such as 'can identify' or 'need not be injective' states the negative conclusion rather than supplying evidence. A non-injectivity claim requires a specific mechanism showing that distinct classes are identified, ideally a demonstrated pair with the same image; absent that, prefer the strongest positive structural statement that is otherwise supported, while still rejecting an embedding claim that carries an unsupported extra hypothesis.
- In consistency or model-existence questions, track each 'it is consistent that' clause and each 'no model satisfying' exclusion separately. An option asserting that a configuration is consistent with an additional axiom is not defeated merely because the combination is not obvious; conversely, an option asserting impossibility or exclusion under an axiom requires affirmative support. When a choice combines a consistency claim with compatibility with an additional axiom such as WUA, evaluate both claims as stated; do not downgrade it to a cautious exclusion merely because the coexistence seems surprising. Do not substitute an unsupported exclusion for a consistency claim, and do not add consistency claims that the option itself does not state.
- When choices partition a parameter into regimes, compare the boundary convention as part of the mathematical claim: endpoint inclusion, use of a strict inequality, and the exact exponent or bound are separate assertions. If two choices differ mainly by one closing an endpoint that the other leaves open, require explicit support for the endpoint and select the open version if the endpoint is not independently supported; broader boundary coverage is not automatically stronger. Likewise, do not treat a sharper-looking exponent as stronger without checking the inequality direction, the lower-bound relation, and the quantifier in each regime, and do not transfer a result across a boundary unless the option and the hypotheses support that transfer.

## Final Answer
- The final-answer instruction means that the text content inside the required <answer> element is a single option label; it never permits a bare label or any text outside that element. Return exactly one <answer>label</answer> element with no surrounding explanation, even when the question asks for a strongest, complete, or equivalent statement. Treat the entire response as that element: begin with <answer>, immediately place exactly one option label, and end with </answer>; if you are about to emit only a letter, wrap it in the element instead. This response-level check is mandatory even when the selected option is a meta-option or when the reasoning was difficult.
