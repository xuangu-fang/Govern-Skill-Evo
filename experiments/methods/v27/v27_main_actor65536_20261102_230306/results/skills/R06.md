# Live Mathematical MCQ Heuristics

## Option Comparison
- Compare all options before committing. The correct choice is often the strongest statement justified by the question, while nearby distractors are weaker, overstrong, or miss an equality case.
- When the question asks for the strongest statement about a structure and one option gives only a base existence or containment assertion while another adds a quantified property to that same object, compare the added property rather than assuming the shorter base assertion is safest. If the added property—such as an almost-everywhere tangent-plane identification or pairwise distinct lengths—is supported by the stated hypotheses at the exact quantifier given, the compound option is stronger than the base-only option. If the added property is unsupported, changes the object (for example, the set itself versus the support of the underlying measure), or upgrades an almost-everywhere claim to an everywhere claim, reject that compound option rather than attributing the base part's support to it.
- Track exact quantifiers such as "there exists", "for every", "if and only if", and "exactly when".
- In a question that explicitly asks for the strongest, complete, or equivalent statement, when an option is a generic meta-claim that one of the remaining options is correct but a stronger result can be proven—even if it does not name the stronger result—treat it as a substantive candidate; do not discard it as non-mathematical, reject it solely because it is nonspecific, or default to a concrete candidate. Evaluate it after the concrete options, and do not select a concrete option merely because it is the most detailed or closest-looking match. Classify each concrete option as false, true but only a necessary condition, one direction, a restricted subclass or auxiliary version, a weaker equivalence, or a full statement of the requested strength. A meta-claim is supported only when comparison shows that a specific concrete option is true but supplies at most a weaker, restricted, one-directional, or otherwise incomplete version of the requested result, and the meta-claim asserts a stronger or equivalent conclusion—or the strongest statement requested by the question—supported by the hypotheses, even if the meta-claim does not name the stronger result. It is not supported merely because the concrete options are hard to verify or because every concrete option is false. Do not reject a supported meta-claim merely because a concrete option is also true when that option is weaker and the meta-claim asserts the requested stronger or equivalent conclusion. In an equivalence question, check both directions: a concrete option supplying only a sufficient condition or only one implication is not a full equivalence. If a concrete option already gives the full requested strength and is supported, select that concrete option rather than the meta-claim. Do not equate truth of a restricted or necessary-only statement with the strongest, equivalent, or complete conclusion requested by the question. Do not reject the meta-claim merely because the question says 'valid' rather than 'strongest.'
- When options share a main claim but one adds a restriction to a proper subclass or substitutes a decorated or auxiliary version of the object, treat that restriction or substitution as an independent claim. Select it only when the question asks about that restricted or auxiliary object and the added claim is supported; a result true for a proper subclass or auxiliary version does not by itself establish the unrestricted or original-object statement. If the question itself concerns the restricted or auxiliary version, evaluate that version directly rather than rejecting it merely because it is more qualified.
- When an option conjoins ordinary unboundedness with a stable or normalized limit claim, evaluate the two assertions separately and use the exact limit in the option. Unbounded ordinary values do not imply that a normalized limit is zero, finite, or positive; a positive stable limit is a distinct stronger assertion, and a vanishing stable-limit claim requires support beyond unboundedness. In particular, when the question asks for the strongest statement and both a vanishing stable-limit option and a positive stable-limit option are present, do not choose the vanishing option merely because ordinary unboundedness is established; compare the exact limiting behavior and select the option whose stable-limit claim is supported. Do not infer either vanishing or positivity from ordinary unboundedness alone.

- In a strongest or equivalence question that contains a meta-claim, compare concrete options clause by clause: an 'at most N' degree clause is weaker than an exact-degree clause only when the remaining clauses match and the exact-degree claim is supported; an exact-degree or more detailed option is not full-strength if its exponent, hypothesis, or quantifier clause is unsupported. A uniform or reordered quantifier such as a single R for every o is not interchangeable with separate 'for every o' and 'for every R' requirements. If a concrete option is true but only weaker, restricted, one-directional, or differently quantified and no concrete option independently gives full requested strength, select the meta-claim rather than defaulting to that concrete option merely because it is explicit; if a concrete option independently gives full strength, select it instead.

## Theorem-Level Precision
- Check whether an option weakens the conclusion by dropping a characterization, equality clause, or full equivalence.
- Check whether an option overstates the theorem by upgrading regularity, removing scale restrictions, or changing an existential statement into a universal one.
- In compound options, treat every added clause—including an added converse, an added failure or impossibility claim, and an added positive existence or consistency claim such as consistency with a named axiom—as an independent claim that can make the whole option false. Select a compound option only when the main clause and every added clause are supported by the question; do not infer such a clause merely from a plausible main statement or because the wording is more cautious or more complete. A request for a complete list, an exact classification, or the strongest statement makes an added completeness or consistency clause relevant, but it still must be independently supported: do not treat "conversely, every such parameter occurs" as established merely because the question asks for a complete list, and do not reject a supported stronger positive clause merely to fall back to a weaker main-clause-only option. Preserve whatever stronger positive statement is actually supported by the hypotheses.
- In options comparing equivalence relations, distinguish full homotopy equivalence from equivalence after localization at each prime or another weaker or localized relation. A localized conclusion does not establish full integral equivalence, and a full-equivalence sufficiency claim requires more than agreement of local invariants; match the exact relation and the direction (necessary condition, sufficiency, or equivalence) asserted by each option. Do not upgrade a localized conclusion to global equivalence, and do not reject a supported localized or necessary-condition statement merely because a stronger global statement would be preferable if it were supported.
- In a piecewise existence or regularity option, check the exact boundaries and form of each exponent condition: do not treat q<... and q≤... as interchangeable, and do not treat an exact exponent formula, a strict upper bound, and an exponent constrained only by an auxiliary inequality as the same claim. A longer or more precise-looking formula is not automatically the strongest valid statement.

- In a complete-classification or compound-option question, if two options differ only by an added converse, apply the independent-clause rule: keep the shared main-clause option when the converse is unsupported, and select the added-converse option when the converse is explicitly supported by the question or a separately supported theorem statement.

## Hypotheses
- Verify the hypotheses and domain carefully. Distractors often keep the theorem shape but alter the required assumptions.
- Pay close attention to equality cases, extremal conditions, and whether a result applies to the full family or only a restricted subfamily.
